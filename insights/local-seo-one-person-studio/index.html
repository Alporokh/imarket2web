Local SEO for a one-person studio: what actually works.

You are one person with a chair, a calendar and maybe two hours a week for marketing. Most local SEO advice is written for businesses with a marketing department. This is the version for you, in the order I would do it.

By Olena Porokh · imarket2web

On this page The uncomfortable bit 1. The profile 2. Categories 3. Reviews 4. Pages 5. Photos What wastes time The order

The uncomfortable bit first

For a studio serving one city, the map pack is worth more than everything else on this page combined. Those three results sit above the organic links, they carry a star rating and a distance, and on a phone they fill most of the first screen.

Which means the honest order of work is: fix the thing that decides map-pack position, then worry about your website. That is the opposite of how most agencies sell it, because a website is a bigger invoice than a profile.

If you have two hours a week, spend the first six weeks entirely on your Google Business Profile and your reviews. Nothing else you can do in that time comes close.

1. The profile, done properly once

Most studio profiles are 60% complete and have been for two years. Completeness is not a vanity metric here - Google uses it to decide whether it knows enough about you to recommend you confidently.

  • Name: your actual business name. Not "Studio Name - Permanent Makeup Poznań Best Prices". Keyword stuffing the name is against the guidelines and competitors can and do report it.
  • Hours, including exceptions. A profile that says open when you are shut produces one-star reviews from people who drove across town.
  • Services, listed individually with prices. Each one is a thing people search for.
  • Attributes: accessibility, parking, languages spoken, appointment required. These feed real filters people use.
  • Booking link, pointed wherever you actually take bookings.

This is an afternoon of work, once. It is the highest-return afternoon in local marketing.

2. Categories: the single highest-leverage field

Your primary category does more to determine what you rank for than anything else in the profile. Get it wrong and the rest of the work fights uphill.

The primary category should be the thing you most want to be found for, not the broadest description of you. A studio that does permanent makeup and nails picks one as primary, based on which pays better - not "Beauty Salon", which puts you in a pool with every salon in the city.

Secondary categories cover the rest. Add the ones that genuinely apply and stop; irrelevant categories dilute rather than add.

How to check what your competitors chose
It is public, if you know where to look

Open a competitor's profile, view the page source, and search for the category strings. It is tedious but it tells you exactly which category the businesses ranking above you are using, which is better evidence than any guess.

3. Reviews: the timing matters more than the wording

Every guide tells you to ask for reviews. Almost none tell you when, and the when is what decides whether you get one.

Ask at the moment the customer is happiest with the result, which for most treatments is not when they walk out the door. For permanent makeup it is after the healing week, when the colour has settled and they have had the first compliment. For massage it is that evening. For a tattoo it is once it has healed and they have shown someone.

Ask by the channel you already use with them. If your booking runs through Booksy or WhatsApp, ask there - not by an email address they gave you once.

  • Reply to every review, including the good ones. Replies are visible to everyone deciding whether to book.
  • Reply to bad ones calmly, in public, once. Then move it to a private channel. The reply is not for the reviewer; it is for the next person reading.
  • Never buy reviews. A sudden burst of five-star reviews from accounts with no history is the pattern Google is best at detecting, and the penalty lands on the profile you depend on.

A steady trickle beats a burst. Ten reviews over ten months is a stronger signal than ten in a week, and it looks more honest to a human reading them.

4. Pages: one per thing people search for

Here is where the website earns its place. The mistake nearly every studio makes is a single price list with every treatment on it. That page can only rank for one thing, and it answers nobody's specific question well.

Someone searching for lip blush and someone searching for removal after someone else's bad work are not the same customer. They have different fears, different budgets and different urgency. One page cannot serve both.

Give each treatment its own page, and write it the way people actually ask:

  • What it is, in their words rather than the trade term
  • Whether it hurts, and what you do about that
  • How long it lasts and what the healing week looks like
  • What it costs - a range is fine, silence is not
  • What happens if they do not like it

Those last three are the questions that arrive in your DMs. Answering them on the page does two jobs: it ranks for the long-tail search, and it removes the doubt that was stopping the booking.

5. Photos, which nobody keeps up

Profiles with recent photos get more engagement, and engagement feeds back into visibility. But the useful version is narrower than "post more photos".

  • The exterior, from the direction people approach. This is the photo that stops someone walking past your door.
  • The room. For a first treatment, the feeling of the space is a large part of the decision.
  • Healed results, not fresh ones. Fresh work photographs better and disappoints later.
  • You. A single-practitioner studio's advantage is that it is you every time. A chain cannot promise that.

What wastes an afternoon

Equally important, and rarely said out loud:

  • Citation building at volume. Paying to be listed in 200 directories was useful a decade ago. Now a handful of real local ones is enough, and the rest is noise.
  • Chasing your rank daily. Local results vary by the searcher's exact location. What you see standing in your own studio is not what a customer sees two districts away.
  • Posting for the sake of it. A Google Business post that supports nothing you sell costs you the twenty minutes and returns nothing.
  • Rewriting your homepage again. If the map pack is where your customers decide, the homepage is not the bottleneck.
  • Buying backlinks. For a local studio these rarely move local rankings and occasionally cause real damage. Earning a handful of genuinely relevant ones is a different piece of work, and a slower one.

The order, if you only have two hours a week

Weeks 1-2: complete the profile, fix the categories
Weeks 3-6: a review habit, asked at the right moment
Weeks 7-12: one page per treatment, best-selling first
Ongoing: photos monthly, reply to every review
Only then: analytics, content calendar, everything else

None of this is quick. All of it compounds, which is the point: the profile you complete this month keeps earning while you are working on the pages, and the reviews you collect keep arriving while you do nothing at all.

One honest caveat
Local SEO is slow, and anyone promising otherwise is selling something

Category changes can move things within weeks. Reviews and pages take months. If someone guarantees you the map pack by a date, ask them what happens when they miss it.
